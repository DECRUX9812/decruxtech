The 30-minute checkup is the whole business model.

If a firm will not look at your setup without a signed agreement, the diagnosis is not the problem they are selling you. The agreement is.

A checkup is cheap to give away because it costs us little and tells us everything. In thirty minutes we learn whether the backups are real, whether the accounts are shared, and whether the person across the table actually wants the problem solved. Roughly half the time, the honest answer is that they need one fix and no ongoing service. Saying that out loud has been better for business than any pitch.

It also sets the terms of the relationship. The roadmap is yours whether or not you hire us, which means the next conversation is about work, not about trust. Any firm that needs a contract before it will tell you what is broken is telling you something about how it earns.

What happens in the thirty minutes

  • How access works today, and who holds the keys
  • What is backed up, and when it was last restored
  • The three things that would hurt most if they failed tomorrow

You leave with those three, ordered. That is the entire deliverable, and it is genuinely useful even if we never speak again.

We do not bill by the ticket.

A per-ticket model pays a firm to let small problems stay small until they are expensive.

Consider the incentive. If every phone call is an invoice line, the customer learns to wait. They batch problems, work around them, and call only when something is properly broken. The firm earns more per incident and less per relationship. Nobody planned this; it is simply what the pricing rewards.

Flat monthly flips it. Prevention is cheaper than repair for us, so it is worth our time to fix the recurring thing permanently rather than close the ticket. It also makes your costs predictable, which matters more to a fifteen-person firm than any discount.

The bill should not change because something broke.

That is the whole argument. Everything else about how we work follows from it.

What HIPA actually asks of a small clinic.

Saskatchewan clinics answer to the Health Information Protection Act, not PIPEDA. That distinction matters more than most software vendors admit.

In practice the obligations land in a few ordinary places. You need to know who can see patient information and be able to show it. You need a custodian who is accountable for it. You need to keep it inside systems you have actually assessed, and you need a plan for the day a laptop goes missing.

None of that requires exotic technology. Most of it requires naming a person, writing down what is true today, and closing the gaps that are indefensible rather than the ones that are merely untidy.

The four that come up every time

  • Shared logins, which make an access record meaningless
  • Backups that exist but have never been restored
  • Patient information in personal email or a phone camera roll
  • No written answer to "what do we do if this machine is stolen"
Not legal advice. This is an operational summary from doing the work, not a legal opinion. Your college or a Saskatchewan lawyer should confirm obligations for your practice.

Own your stack, or rent your constraints.

Every rented tool is a decision someone else can reverse on your behalf.

Subscription infrastructure is usually the right call. It is cheaper, faster and better maintained than anything a small firm would build. The trouble starts when the rental becomes the business: the client list, the booking system, the records, the identity that everything else trusts.

The test we apply is simple. If this vendor doubled its price, changed its terms or disappeared next quarter, what would we still have? If the answer is "an export nobody can read", that is not a technology decision, it is a dependency.

Where we draw the line

  • Records and client data: yours, exportable, on infrastructure you control
  • Identity: your own domain, your own admin account
  • Website and email: portable, with a documented way to move
  • Everything else: rent happily, as long as the export works
